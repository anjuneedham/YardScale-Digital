Chapter 01 — Is it right for you?Design Container JA
Chapter 01

Is a container home
right for you?

The honest answer is that it depends almost entirely on your land and your timeline — not on the container.

Container construction solves one problem extremely well: it moves the build off your site and into a yard, where weather, theft and labour availability stop dictating the schedule. A block-and-steel house in Jamaica routinely runs eighteen months and a budget that drifts the whole way. A container home is fabricated in ten to fourteen weeks while your foundation goes in, then set in a day.

That is the real product. Everything else — the sustainability story, the modern look — is secondary, and you should be suspicious of anyone selling it the other way round.

It works well when

  • You already own land with clear registered title
  • A low-bed truck and a crane can reach the building position
  • You want a fixed price you can actually hold someone to
  • You need to be in within a year, not three
  • You are building a second unit, a rental, or a guest cottage
  • You expect to extend later and want that designed in from the start

It works badly when

  • The site cannot take a crane and there is no alternative access
  • You want more than about 1,200 sq ft — the economics flatten out
  • Your title is unclear or the land is family property in dispute
  • You want a conventional mortgage and nothing else will do
  • You are on a steep, unstable slope with no engineered platform
  • You want wide, open interior spans without visible structure
The single biggest predictor

Access. We have quoted beautiful sites that could not take delivery, and unremarkable ones that were straightforward. Before you fall in love with a model, stand at your gate and ask whether a 40-foot low-bed could get in, turn, and whether a crane could set up within reach of the building position. Chapter 09 gives you a checklist for exactly this.

Chapter 02

What it actually costs

The unit price is the number everyone quotes. It is rarely more than two thirds of what you will spend.

Container homes in Jamaica are widely advertised from around J$2.5M for a one-bedroom to roughly J$12.5M for a three-bedroom, with a 480 sq ft two-bedroom — typically a 20-foot and a 40-foot high-cube combined — sitting near the middle of that range. Those are unit prices. They are real, and they are not the whole story.

Here is everything that sits behind the headline figure. Not all of it will apply to you, but you should be able to say why for each line.

The rule of thumb

On a straightforward site with services close by, budget the unit price plus about 35–50% for everything else. On a remote or steep site with no services at the boundary, that figure can approach 100%. Any builder who will not talk about this openly is telling you something.

What "finished" should mean

The word does a lot of work in this industry. Before you compare two quotations, make each one confirm — in writing — whether the following are inside the price.

INTERIOR

Should be included

Insulation, interior wall and ceiling lining, flooring, internal doors, kitchen cabinetry and counter, bathroom suite, tiling to wet areas, wardrobe or closet joinery, and all internal painting.

SERVICES

Should be included

Full electrical first and second fix to a consumer unit, all plumbing to a single connection point, water heater, extract ventilation to the bathroom, and provision for air conditioning even if units are not fitted.

EXTERIOR

Ask specifically

Roof canopy, gutters and downpipes, external doors and glazing with cyclone-rated fixings, the full coating system, deck or steps, and any external lighting.

PAPERWORK

Ask specifically

Stamped structural drawings, the engineer's certification, as-built drawings at handover, warranty documents, and the compliance schedule your parish council submission needs.

Three things that are almost never included

  • Air conditioning units themselves. Provision is usually in; the equipment usually is not.
  • Solar panels and inverter. A good build is solar-ready — roof rated for the array, conduit run, inverter position pre-wired. The hardware is a separate purchase.
  • Furniture, white goods and window treatments. Obvious when written down, routinely forgotten in a budget.
A question worth asking

"If I sign today, what is the total figure I will have paid you by handover, assuming nothing changes?" A builder working to a genuine fixed price can answer that in one number. If the answer needs paragraphs, you are looking at a provisional sum, not a price.

Chapter 04

Importing it yourself

Overseas factories advertise container homes from US$8,500. That number is true and almost completely misleading. Here is why.

A prefabricated building imported into Jamaica is classified under HS heading 9406. On arrival it attracts, in order:

  • Customs duty at 20%, calculated on the CIF value — the cost of the goods plus insurance plus freight, not just what you paid the factory.
  • General Consumption Tax at 15%, calculated on the duty-inclusive value. GCT is charged on top of the duty, not alongside it.
  • Stamp duty on the import, plus the standard customs administration fee.

Because GCT compounds on the duty, the effective uplift on CIF lands around 35–38% before the unit leaves the wharf.

Chapter 04 — Importing it yourselfDesign Container JA
Confirm before you commit

Rates and classifications change, and a misclassification is expensive to unwind at the wharf. Have a licensed customs broker price your shipment in writing before you pay a factory deposit. This example is illustrative, not a quotation.

The four risks nobody mentions in the brochure

01The specification is written for a different climate

Most exported units are built to a temperate specification. Insulation is thin or absent, the coating system is not marine grade, and the fixings are not rated for a Caribbean gust. You will pay to correct all three, and correcting insulation after lining is fitted means stripping the interior.

02There is no enforceable warranty

A warranty from a factory eight thousand miles away, governed by a foreign jurisdiction, is worth what it costs you to enforce. Ask yourself who you would actually call about a leak in year three.

03It may not certify for approval

Parish council will want structural drawings and evidence of compliance with the Building Act 2018. A foreign unit rarely arrives with anything a local registered engineer can certify without a full assessment — which you then pay for, and which may require modification.

04Lenders will not touch it

Without a stamped engineer's report, an imported shell is not financeable in practice. You will be funding the whole thing from cash.

When importing does make sense

If you are buying several units, have an engineer and a broker you already work with, and are prepared to treat the import as a shell to be substantially rebuilt locally — the economics can work. For a single family home, they usually do not, once the corrections above are priced honestly.

Chapter 05 — ApprovalsDesign Container JA
Chapter 05

Approvals

A container home sited permanently is a building. It needs approval like any other building, and the process is not unusually hard — it is just unusually easy to start late.

For a single dwelling you are applying to your parish council for building approval. Larger or environmentally sensitive developments go to NEPA. Your submission must demonstrate compliance with the Building Act 2018 and the wind loading requirements of the Caribbean Uniform Building Code (CUBiC).

What a complete submission contains

  • Site plan showing boundaries, setbacks, the building position and access
  • Floor plans, elevations and sections of the proposed dwelling
  • Structural drawings and calculations, stamped by a registered engineer
  • Foundation and anchorage detail — how the unit ties to the ground
  • Drainage and sewage disposal proposals, including percolation where relevant
  • Proof of ownership or the owner's written consent
  • The application form and the parish fee, which scales with floor area
Start it early — it runs in parallel

Approval commonly takes six to twelve weeks. Fabrication takes ten to fourteen. Those two clocks should run at the same time, not one after the other. A builder who waits for approval before starting fabrication has quietly added three months to your project.

The four things that hold an approval up

01Title that is not clean

Family land held in common, an unregistered transfer, or a boundary that does not match the survey plan will stop an application before it is assessed. This is the most common cause of delay and the slowest to fix — start here, months before anything else.

02No stamped structural design

Drawings of a container are not a structural design. The council wants calculations for wind uplift and the anchorage detail, signed by a registered engineer who is prepared to stand behind them.

03Sewage disposal not resolved

Where there is no sewer, you need a workable absorption solution and, on tight or rocky sites, evidence it will actually perform. Councils reject vague answers here routinely.

04Setbacks and coverage

A 40-foot unit is long. On a narrow lot it is easy to breach a side setback without noticing until the site plan is drawn properly. Check this before you choose a model, not after.

Chapter 06 — Paying for itDesign Container JA
Chapter 06

Paying for it

This is where most container home projects actually stall. Not at the council, and not at the yard — at the bank.

Conventional mortgage lending in Jamaica is built around block-and-steel construction on registered land. Prefabricated housing sits outside that comfort zone, and most commercial banks will decline or offer terms that make the project pointless. That is the honest position, and any builder who tells you otherwise is hoping you will find out later.

What does work, in rough order of how often we see it:

01

Credit union facility

Several Jamaican credit unions will lend against prefabricated housing where the member has a solid record. Usually structured as a secured personal or home-improvement loan rather than a mortgage. Shorter term, higher rate, far more achievable.

02

Staged self-funding

Buy a studio outright, live in it, add a module when you can. This is genuinely practical with container construction in a way it is not with conventional building, provided the structure is designed for extension from day one.

03

Rental-backed

Short-let or long-let income services the borrowing. Works where the location genuinely supports occupancy — and you should model it with pessimistic assumptions, not the good months.

What a lender will ask you for

  • Clear registered title to the land, in your name, unencumbered
  • A stamped engineer's report on the structure and its foundation
  • The approved parish council submission, or evidence it is lodged
  • A valuation from a valuer prepared to assess prefabricated construction
  • A fixed-price contract with a defined scope and payment schedule
  • Proof of income and your existing debt commitments, as with any facility
Get the report before you need it

The stamped engineer's report is the document that turns "an interesting box" into "a financeable asset" in a lender's eyes. Ask any builder whether it is included in their price, and whether you will receive it before completion or at handover. Before is far more useful.

Insurance

Insurers price what they understand. A container home with a stamped structural design, a documented anchorage detail and a certified coating system is straightforward to insure. The same home without that paperwork can be difficult and expensive.

When you approach an insurer, lead with the engineering, not the aesthetics. Give them the wind rating the structure was designed to, the hold-down detail, and the engineer's certification. Ask specifically about hurricane cover and what the excess is — that is the clause that matters on this island.

A sensible payment schedule

Fixed-price container contracts are usually staged. A structure that protects both sides looks roughly like this:

StageShareReleased when
Deposit on signing20–25%Contract and drawing set signed by both parties
Start of fabrication25–30%Containers procured, cutting and framing begun
First fix complete20–25%Insulation, electrical and plumbing rough-in inspected
Ready for delivery20–25%Unit finished and inspected in the yard, before it leaves
Retention on handover5–10%Snag list closed, warranty and as-builts handed over
Never pay the full amount before delivery

Hold a retention until the snag list is closed and you have the documents in your hand. Five to ten per cent is normal, fair, and the only leverage you have on the last ten per cent of quality. A builder confident in their work will not object to it.

Chapter 07

Building for this climate

A shipping container is designed to be stacked nine high and thrown across the Atlantic. That makes it a good starting point and a poor finished house. Four things have to change.

01 — Wind

The steel box is not the weak point; the connection to the ground is. Uplift is what destroys buildings in a hurricane, and a container that is simply set on blocks will move. What you want to see is a cast reinforced foundation with engineered hold-down plates at every corner casting, designed to CUBiC wind loading — a 150 mph design gust is a reasonable benchmark — and certified by a registered structural engineer.

Ask to see the anchorage detail drawing. If one does not exist, the design does not exist.

02 — Heat

An uninsulated container in Jamaican sun is genuinely unbearable, and this is where cheap units fail hardest. Two things fix it, and you need both:

  • Closed-cell spray foam applied to the inside face of the steel, typically 50 mm. Closed-cell matters — it insulates and it stops warm humid air reaching the cold steel and condensing behind your lining. Batt insulation in a stud cavity does not do the second job, and the result is mould you cannot see.
  • A ventilated roof canopy over the container. Keeping direct sun off the box entirely does more for comfort than any air conditioner, and it costs less to run for the next twenty years.
The condensation test

Ask a builder how they stop condensation forming on the inside face of the steel. If the answer is about ventilation or a vapour barrier rather than closed-cell foam bonded directly to the steel, keep asking. This is the single most common failure in container housing, and it shows up in year two, behind the wall, where you cannot see it.

03 — Salt and corrosion

A used container arrives with a marine coating that has already done its working life, plus whatever rust came with it. Painting over that buys you a season or two. Proper preparation is:

  • Shot-blast or mechanically abrade back to bright steel, treating any pitting
  • Zinc-rich primer across the whole shell
  • Two-pack polyurethane topcoat — the system used on offshore plant and bridges
  • All cut edges and welds treated before the topcoat, not after

Expect to recoat every ten to twelve years near the coast. That is a weekend's work on a well-prepared shell and a rebuild on a badly prepared one.

04 — Water

Every opening cut into a container is a potential leak, because cutting a corrugated wall removes structure as well as steel. Each opening should be framed and welded, then sealed — not sealed alone. Ask how openings are framed; the answer tells you most of what you need to know about the rest of the build.

Above that, you want a roof that actually sheds water away from the box with gutters and downpipes to a tank connection, and a deck detail that does not trap standing water against the steel.

ElementAcceptableWhat to avoid
AnchorageEngineered hold-downs at corner castings, cast foundation, certifiedSet on blocks or an uncertified slab
Insulation50 mm closed-cell foam bonded to the steelBatt insulation in a stud cavity
CoatingBlast, zinc-rich primer, two-pack polyurethanePaint applied over the existing finish

Chapter 08 — Before you pay a depositDesign Container JA
Chapter 08

Seven questions before
you pay a deposit

Ask all seven. Write the answers down. A builder worth your money will answer every one without hesitating, and will not mind you asking.

01Can I see a completed unit and speak to the owner?

Never buy a container home you have not stood inside. Photographs hide ceiling height, condensation staining, door quality and how the space actually feels at midday. And the owner will tell you things the builder will not.

02Who is the registered engineer, and will I get the stamped drawings?

You want a name you can verify and a commitment that the stamped structural drawings and certification come to you — ideally before completion, since that is what your lender and insurer will want to see.

03What exactly is inside this price, and what is not?

Ask for it as a written scope, not a conversation. Use the checklist in Chapter 02. Pay particular attention to the foundation, services connections, approval fees and delivery radius.

04How do you insulate, and how do you stop condensation on the steel?

The answer should be closed-cell spray foam bonded directly to the inside face. Anything else, keep asking. This is the question that most reliably separates a builder from an assembler.

05What is the coating system, and what preparation comes first?

You are listening for blasting back to bare steel, a zinc-rich primer and a two-pack topcoat. "We paint it" is not a coating system.

06What happens if you are late, and what happens if I am?

A real contract addresses both. If the builder's delays carry no consequence but yours carry a penalty, you have learned something useful about the relationship.

07What is the warranty, and who honours it in year four?

Ask for the term on the structure, on the coating, and on the fit-out separately — they are rarely the same. Then ask who you call. A warranty is only as good as the company still being there.

Five answers that should worry you

"We don't need approval for this"

A permanently sited dwelling needs building approval. A builder who tells you otherwise is either inexperienced or is planning to hand you a problem that surfaces when you try to sell, insure or borrow against the property.

"The price depends on final quantities"

That is a provisional sum wearing a fixed price's clothes. Ask what the number will be if nothing changes. If they cannot say, you do not have a price.

"Insulation is included" — with no further detail

Insulation type matters more than its presence. Get the material and the thickness in writing.

"We need the full amount before it leaves the yard"

Retention exists for a reason. A builder unwilling to leave five per cent on the table until snags are closed is telling you what they expect the snag list to look like.

"That's just how containers are"

Used to explain condensation, heat, noise or rust. None of those are inherent to container construction — they are symptoms of a specification that was cut somewhere. Ask where.

Chapter 09 — Site-readiness checklistDesign Container JA
Chapter 09

Your site-readiness
checklist

Print this page and walk your land with it. Twenty minutes here will save you a great deal later, and it is the fastest way to make a first meeting with any builder productive.

Access — the one that decides everything

Can a 40 ft low-bed truck reach the property?Check the narrowest point, the tightest bend, and any low branches or wires along the route.
Is there room to turn or reverse near the site?A truck that can get in but not out is a truck that never comes in.
Can a crane set up within reach of the building position?It needs firm level ground and clear airspace overhead — power lines are the usual obstruction.
Are there weight-restricted bridges or culverts on the route?Worth a phone call to the parish council before you assume.

Land and title

Is the title registered and in your name?If it is family land held in common, start resolving that now — it takes the longest of anything in this guide.
Do you have a current survey plan?You will need it for the site plan, and boundaries on the ground often differ from memory.
Does the building position respect the setbacks?A 40 ft unit is long. Measure the side boundaries before choosing a model.
Is any part of the site in a flood-prone or gully-adjacent area?This affects both approval and insurance.

Ground

How much fall is there across the building position?Gentle slope is fine and often an advantage. Steep slope means retaining or piling, and a materially different foundation cost.
Is there rock at or near the surface?Affects foundation, septic absorption and cost. Look at any existing cut or excavation nearby.

Services

Where is the nearest JPS pole, and how far from the building position?Distance drives the connection cost. Pace it out and write the number down.
Is there an NWC water main at the boundary?If not, plan for a tank and pump, and budget accordingly.
Is there a sewer connection, or will you need a septic system?Most sites outside town need septic. It needs space and it needs ground that will absorb.
Where will surface water go in heavy rain?Stand on the site and work out the fall. This matters more than people expect.

Orientation and comfort

Which way does the prevailing breeze come from?Orienting the long axis to catch it is free comfort, permanently. Get this wrong and no amount of air conditioning fixes it cheaply.
Where does the afternoon sun fall?You want the largest glazing away from the worst of it, or shaded by the canopy.
Is there existing shade worth keeping?A mature tree is worth designing around.
